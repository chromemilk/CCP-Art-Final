13|Vitruvian Man|Leonardo da Vinci|1490|talian High Renaissance|Sketch|Gallerie dell'Accademia|At first glance, the work may seem like a simple representation of the human form—however the secret lies in the circle and the square. During the High Renaissance, there was a challenge given by the Roman architect Vitruvius, who proposed that a well-built man could fit perfectly into a square and a circle. Leonardo noticed that the naval of the figure should be the center of the circle, while the genitals should be the center of the square.|From a mathematical point of view, this “solved” the impossible problem of finding a square with the same area as a circle using only a straight-edge and compass. However, Leonardo’s approach involved using the ratios of the human body to approximate the positions needed to get finitely close to the answer. Beyond that, it was also anatomically impressive. Specifically, you can see the deliberate muscular outlines of this ideal figure around the waist and knees, representing Leonardo’s dedication to the empirical study of the human body. It can also be seen that, depending on the angle of the arms, they can both perfectly reach either side of the square, or become a faux radius of the circle, further “proving” the mathematical theorem.|Overall, this piece bridges the gap between art, science, and the mathematics of the human body. It successfully portrays the anatomically correct figure of the ideal man while simultaneously “proving” an impossible geometric theorem. This kind of abstract thinking is what distinguishes Leonardo from the other artists, and it served to create one of the most well-known examples of the human form.|man.bmp|18.5|6.9
